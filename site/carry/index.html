How it earns, in plain words

Futures exchanges keep their "perpetual" futures close to the coin's real price with a funding payment: every few hours (usually 8), one side pays the other a small percentage. When more traders bet on prices rising, which is most of the time, the buyers of the future pay the sellers.

This account takes the seller's side without betting on the price: for each coin it buys the coin and sells the same amount of its future. If the price rises, the coin gains what the future loses; if it falls, the reverse. The price moves cancel out, and what is left is the funding payment.

Example: $10 of Bitcoin held and $10 of the Bitcoin future sold. At a typical funding rate of +0.01% every 8 hours, the account receives $0.001 three times a day: about 11% a year on the $10, but the account also keeps $10 as the future's margin, so about 5% a year on the $20 it ties up, before fees.

The rules and the research behind them

Loading…

Checked on 3 months of OKX history (13 July to 14 October 2026), after fees:

  • Holding the pair through earned about 3% to 7% a year in funding on major coins (Bitcoin about 5%), and funding was positive 70% to 90% of the time.
  • Switching between coins to chase the highest rate lost money: every switch pays fees on both sides. So the rules hold steadily and only leave a coin when its funding turns negative.
  • One coin's funding (WAL) went sharply negative, about −24% a year, which is why the basket is only large, heavily traded coins.

Risks, honestly

  • Funding can turn negative, and then the seller pays. The account leaves a coin once its last 7 days add up to a negative number, but it pays the fees to do so.
  • A sharp price rise makes the futures side lose before the coin side is sold. With the margin equal to the position (no leverage), the price would have to rise about 70% before the risk rule closes the pair; on a real exchange the margin rules may be stricter.
  • The two prices aren't identical: the future can trade a little above or below the coin, so opening and closing cost a small gap, shown in every trade.
  • The exchange itself holds both sides: if it failed, both could be lost. Spreading across exchanges reduces this but adds cost.
  • Where you live matters. OKX, used for the prices here, does not serve US residents. In the US, perpetual-style futures are offered by regulated venues such as Coinbase Financial Markets, with their own fees and funding. Check with a licensed adviser before copying anything with real money, and remember that funding income is taxable.
  • This is income of a few percent a year, not a way to multiply money. It is shown because it is one of the few edges that doesn't depend on predicting prices.
